Course Description

Environmental ethics investigates our relationship to the natural world and asks how we should respond to problems that arise from that relationship. This course focuses on the most pressing version of that relationship for our generation: climate change. This is an especially difficult moral problem because its causes are collective and its effects are not distributed equally across the world or across generations. Finally, and perhaps most pressingly, many feel helpless in the face of this problem because it seems like no one individual is capable of making much difference.

In this course, we will philosophically examine questions about justice, responsibility, and solidarity in the face of climate change, and explore questions about the moral value of the non-human world and controversial technological options such as geoengineering. Throughout the semester, we’ll consider these problems in the light of philosophical work on the nature of morality. Over the course of the semester, students will develop a more sophisticated conceptual vocabulary to engage with environmental issues and think about the ethical concerns that spring from them.

Our Jobs In This Course

My job in this course is to provide you with some important conceptual tools, to facilitate interesting conversations, and to grade your performance. Your job in this course is, at a minimum, to keep up with the readings, to understand the readings and associated course content, and to demonstrate your understanding in discussion, presentations, and oral examinations.

Even if you’ve already taken a higher-level philosophy course with me, you should prepare yourself for a challenging, reading-and-discussion-intensive 14 weeks.

Learning Objectives: Moral and Ethical Discernment

Moral and ethical discernment is defined as a non-judgmental understanding of how moral and ethical standards are formed, how they influence aspects of our lives, and how they shape public discourse and policy. Moral and ethical discernment is linked to such concepts as integrity, objectivity, public interest, and justice.

Class Participation

Class participation is crucial. Students should have the assigned readings completed before each session and be prepared to participate in class. Discussion is an essential component of the philosophical process. A lot of the material will be challenging, so students are encouraged to ask questions. If you don’t understand something, it’s likely that others are also having difficulty with it. Don’t be afraid to ask. Borderline grades may be adjusted in both directions in relation to the quality of a student’s participation.

1. Oral Examinations

There will be two oral examinations — a midterm and a cumulative final — each about 15 minutes, one-on-one with me, and each worth 15% of your course grade. I’ll tell you which readings and topics are in scope for each exam at least a week in advance. We’ll have a 5-minute practice round (worth 5% of your grade) on September 29; this will give everyone a chance to get used to the format.

These exams test whether you can reconstruct an argument, apply it, and hold a position under pressure — not verbal fluency or speed. Pausing, taking notes, asking me to repeat a question, and correcting yourself are totally okay with me and won’t affect your grade.

I know that for some of you, this could be a great source of anxiety. I get it! Answering questions on the spot can be stressful in a way that answering them on a page isn’t. But we’ll get plenty of practice throughout the semester: this is a very small class, so if you speak up, raise your hand to answer my questions, ask questions of your own, it won’t be that big of a deal to sit down with me for a few minutes to talk about what we’ve been studying.

Your final oral exam will be individually scheduled during finals week (December 14–18).

2. Individual Presentation

At some point during the course of the semester, you will give the class a 15-minute presentation on a contemporary article, event, policy, technology, or controversy of your choosing. Your job is to explain it, connect it explicitly to a normative problem from the course, then pose questions and lead the discussion. You can use a PowerPoint or write stuff on the blackboard, do an interpretive dance while you talk — whatever helps you get your ideas across. But none of that is required. What I’m most interested in is whether or not you can clearly explain the issue, tie it to some moral concern, and lead a discussion with your peers.

Be sure to clear your topic with me at least a week before your presentation. I’ll pass around a sign-up sheet with dates early in the semester. I’ll also talk about how I assess your work in more depth, so you get a really clear idea what I’m looking for.

3. Reading Assignments

In class, before any particular reading is due, I’ll hand out one-page reading assignment worksheets that you will fill in and bring to class the next time we meet. The point of these is to confirm that you’ve completed the reading and can identify its main ideas. It’ll also give you a chance to comment and ask questions about things that caught your attention or left you a little bit confused.

The mechanics: each worksheet is due at the start of the class where we discuss that reading — the due date is what matters, not the handout day. If you’re absent when I hand one out, email me and I’ll get you a copy. If you know you’ll miss a class where one is due, turn it in early or contact me in advance with a valid reason; otherwise, work that isn’t turned in counts as missing.

Each worksheet gets one of three marks:

  • ✓+ — complete, accurate, thoughtful engagement with the reading
  • ✓ — substantially complete and demonstrates adequate understanding
  • ✓− — incomplete, superficial, or contains significant misunderstandings

If you don’t turn in the work, you do get a zero. A semester of consistently satisfactory work (✓) will get you a B+/A− in this category. Check-pluses raise that grade and check-minuses lower it; if you get some of each, they’ll generally offset one another. Missing assignments are a much bigger problem than check-minuses. I will drop your two lowest submitted reading assignments, but missing assignments cannot be dropped.

4. Quizzes, Exercises & Coursework

This category is a deliberate mix: pop quizzes, in-class exercises, short homework — whatever this particular class turns out to need. With ten people in the room, I can see what’s working and what isn’t, and this is the part of the grade that lets me respond to that instead of following a script I wrote in August.

I will randomly give pop quizzes, worksheets, homework assignments, and so on, covering the material we’ve read and things we’ve learned in class. These will be used to make sure you have been paying attention and following along. Missing a pop quiz or an assignment will result in a 0% on that assignment. The only exception to this rule is in cases when a student contacts me before class with an acceptable reason for not attending. The lowest grade in this category will be dropped, so everyone gets one chance to mess up.

Progress Grades

Progress grades are based on your performance on coursework completed through the midpoint of the semester. They are intended to provide timely feedback on your academic progress and may not reflect your final course grade. As assignments are completed and graded, grades will be available to check in Blackboard. Progress grades at the midterm will be available in the Self-Service.

In PHI 310, progress grades are submitted on Monday, November 2, and your progress grade usually represents about half of your final grade. By that point you will have taken the practice oral and the midterm oral examination, turned in about half of the reading assignments, and done whatever quizzes and in-class work the semester has called for. If you have already given your individual presentation, that counts toward the progress grade too — so the figure runs a little higher for students who present early and a little lower for those who present late.

Academic Integrity and AI Policy

Academic dishonesty, of which cheating, plagiarism, and unauthorized AI use are the most common examples, is a serious violation of the principles of higher education. Daemen University takes the position that academic honesty is to be upheld with the highest degree of integrity. Please familiarize yourself with the policy in the Daemen academic regulations and standards.

All cases of academic dishonesty will be dealt with very seriously. Plagiarized work, whether intentional or not, will receive an “F” and may result in an “F” for the course. In addition, the student’s name will be submitted to the Associate Dean’s office. In the case of subsequent offenses, a college committee will determine further repercussions, including possible expulsion from the institution.

Using AI in This Course

I think about AI quite a lot. I chair Daemen’s AI task force, and I’ve recently started doing research in the philosophy of AI. So when I say I’m not against the use of AI, I mean it.

In this course, feel free to use AI tools like ChatGPT to brainstorm ideas, organize your thinking, test your understanding of the reading, and copy-edit your writing on homework assignments. But in this course, you may not use AI to generate any content that you actually turn in. Don’t copy and paste anything. If you hand something in to me, be sure that it’s in your own voice, even if it’s rough around the edges.

Take special care with how you use AI on the reading assignments and on the presentations that you give to the class. If you do use AI in either of those, please be sure to let me know what you used and how you used it.

How I Use AI in This Course

I use tools like ChatGPT and Claude every day to inform my teaching, communication, and feedback.

  • Course announcements, syllabus language, and emails may be written or edited with AI support.
  • I sometimes use AI to help draft or organize feedback, but I anonymize all work (no names or identifying information) before doing so. If you’d prefer I not use AI when reviewing your work, email me and I’ll opt you out.
  • Whether feedback or messages are AI-assisted or not, I review everything and take full responsibility for what you receive.

When used effectively, AI can augment your thinking and help you do more. When it replaces your voice, it disempowers you. This policy is meant to show you what empowered AI use looks like — not just what is permissible, but what is worthwhile.
